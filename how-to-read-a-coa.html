How to Read a Certificate of Analysis

A certificate of analysis (COA) is a laboratory's answer to a specific set of questions about one sample. Reading one well means knowing which questions were asked, and which were not.

Identity, purity and content: three different questions

Most peptide COAs answer some combination of three questions. They are measured differently and they fail independently. A sample can be the right molecule at high purity and still be short on content, or hold the stated amount of a less pure material.

QuestionOn the reportUsual methodWhat the figure means
Is it the right molecule?IdentityMass spectrometry (MS, LC-MS, MS/MS)The measured molecular mass matches the mass expected from the sequence or formula.
How much of what was detected is that molecule?Purity (%)HPLC with UV detectionThe main compound's share of the total peak area in the chromatogram.
How much is in the vial?Content / amount (mg or IU)HPLC against a reference standardThe quantity of the compound measured in the vial.

A report answers only the questions the laboratory was asked. That is why the COA Library shows a purity figure only where one was actually reported, and says so plainly where a report measured identity and amount alone.

Identity: mass spectrometry

A mass spectrometer ionizes the sample and measures the mass-to-charge ratio of what it produces. Peptides usually carry several charges at once, so one molecule shows up as a series of peaks that resolve to a single molecular mass. That mass is compared with the theoretical value; the molecular weight listed on each of our product specification sheets is the figure to check it against.

  • A match within the instrument's tolerance confirms the sample contains the expected molecule.
  • A mass that is off by a whole amino-acid residue, or a dominant unexpected mass, points to a different, truncated or modified peptide.
  • LC-MS separates the sample by chromatography first, so the mass of each peak can be checked individually. MS/MS (tandem mass spectrometry, as in UPLC-MS/MS) also fragments the molecule, which gives sequence-level evidence beyond the intact mass.

Purity: HPLC peak area

Reversed-phase HPLC separates the components of a sample as they pass through a column. A UV detector records them as peaks, usually at 214–220 nm, where the peptide bond absorbs. Several of our reports use a photodiode-array (PDA) detector at 220 nm, which records a full UV spectrum across each peak. Purity is the main peak's area divided by the total area of all integrated peaks.

Read a purity figure as "of what this method could see, this share was the main compound." It has three limits worth knowing:

  • It is relative, not absolute. Counter-ions such as acetate or trifluoroacetate, residual water and salts barely absorb at these wavelengths, so they are invisible to it. A vial can be 99% pure by HPLC while its net peptide content is lower than the gross weight of the powder.
  • It only counts what it separates. An impurity that elutes under the main peak, or does not absorb UV, is not counted against purity.
  • Area is not mass. Different compounds absorb UV differently, so area percent approximates weight percent rather than equalling it.

Content: how much is in the vial

Content (also called amount or assay) is quantified against a reference standard of known concentration and reported as mass per vial, or in International Units for somatropin. It is the figure to compare with the quantity on the label.

  • Above the label is common, because vials are usually filled with a margin over the stated amount.
  • Below the label is a real shortfall. Where one of our lots measured below a stated amount, the listing and the report scope table say so, rather than leaving it for you to find in the document.

Multi-peptide blends

In a co-lyophilized blend such as the KLOW Blend or the BPC-157 / TB-500 Blend, each component is identified and quantified separately. A single purity percentage is ambiguous for a blend, because every component takes up part of the same chromatogram. Some laboratories report a combined figure (the summed area of all the declared components). Others report none and give identity and content per component. The COA Library shows whichever the laboratory actually reported, and marks a blend's purity as not applicable where none was given.

Potency in International Units

Recombinant somatropin is conventionally expressed in International Units (IU) as well as milligrams, defined against a World Health Organization reference standard. The conventional conversion is about 3 IU per milligram, so a report that states both figures lets you check one against the other. The HGH 36 IU report also gives a percentage for dimer and related proteins: the share of the protein present as aggregates or modified forms rather than the intact monomer.

Reagents and solvent analyses

A laboratory reagent is not a peptide, so identity and peptide purity do not apply to it in the same way. The report on file for Bacteriostatic Water is a solvent analysis: it measures the preservative (benzyl alcohol) content of the lot. Sterility and bacterial endotoxin are separate microbiological tests, and a solvent analysis says nothing about either.

Dates, batches and scope

  • Test date and report date. In the COA Library, the test date is when the laboratory received the sample and the report date is when the analysis was completed and the report issued.
  • Batch or lot number. A report describes the sample that was tested, which stands for one production batch. Match the batch on the report to the lot of the material you receive.
  • Scope. The vial a laboratory analyzed can differ in size or composition from the listing it supports. Where ours does, the difference is stated on the product page and in the report scope table.
  • What a COA is not. Unless it says so, a COA is not a sterility or endotoxin certificate, not a stability study, and not a statement about what the material is suitable for beyond what was measured.
